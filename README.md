          Elon Musk's ambition to make humanity a multiplanetary species raises a question
          that reaches far beyond rockets and space travel: while some people are working
          to build humanity's future beyond Earth, are developing countries preparing their
          people to participate in that future?
        </p>

        <p>
          The challenge is not simply a lack of money or technology. It also involves
          education, economic opportunity, social development, institutions, work habits,
          critical thinking and the willingness to improve systems that no longer serve people well.
        </p>

        <div class="callout">
          Are we preparing people merely to survive today's challenges, or equipping them

        <h2 id="ambition">1. The ambition to live beyond Earth</h2>
        <p>
          Space exploration represents a long-term human ambition: to expand scientific
          knowledge, develop new technologies and investigate whether people can live
          beyond Earth. Elon Musk and SpaceX are prominent participants in this effort,
          but a permanent settlement on another world remains an exceptionally difficult
          engineering and human challenge.
        </p>
        <p>
          Reaching another planet, establishing a settlement and building a self-sustaining
          civilisation are not the same achievement. Each requires different levels of
          technology, resources, planning and cooperation.
        </p>
        <p>
          That distinction gives us a useful way to think about development on Earth:
          having a vision is important, but creating the conditions that turn a vision into
          a durable reality is equally important.
        </p>

        <h2 id="ecosystem">2. Big ambitions need an ecosystem</h2>
        <p>
          Major engineering projects do not depend on one person's motivation alone.
          They require engineers, scientists, technicians, suppliers, investment,
          infrastructure, education and institutions that can coordinate complex work.
        </p>
        <p>
          The lesson for developing countries is not to copy one entrepreneur. It is to ask
          what kind of environment allows people with ideas and skills to test them, learn
          from failure, build useful products and solve difficult problems.
        </p>
        <ul>
          <li>Set clear goals and measure progress.</li>
          <li>Build practical skills alongside theoretical knowledge.</li>
          <li>Reward useful work, experimentation and problem-solving.</li>
          <li>Make it easier for capable people to start and grow businesses.</li>
          <li>Use evidence to improve plans instead of defending every decision.</li>
        </ul>

        <h2 id="economy">3. Economic readiness: creating opportunities</h2>
        <p>
          People find it difficult to plan for the distant future when they face immediate
          insecurity. Reliable access to food, housing, healthcare, electricity, internet
          and education can influence how much time and energy people can devote to learning
          and building something new.
        </p>
        <p>
          Developing countries are diverse, so there is no single explanation for their
          economic challenges. Relevant issues may include productivity, access to finance,
          infrastructure, public services, corruption risks, market access and the quality
          of available jobs. These factors should be examined with country-specific evidence.
        </p>
        <p>
          A future-ready economy needs more than a small group of highly skilled specialists.
          It needs broad opportunities for people to gain skills, apply them, earn a fair
          living and contribute to useful work.
        </p>

        <h2 id="education">4. Education, work ethic and critical thinking</h2>
        <p>
          Education should help people do more than remember information for an examination.
          It should also help them ask good questions, test claims, communicate clearly,
          work with others and use knowledge to solve real problems.
        </p>
        <p>
          A strong work ethic is not simply working longer hours. It can include reliability,
          attention to quality, persistence, willingness to learn and the ability to adapt
          when evidence shows that a method is not working.
        </p>
        <p>
          Critical thinking is equally important. People should be able to question public
          figures, business leaders, teachers and their own assumptions without treating
          disagreement as a personal attack.
        </p>
        <h3>What could improve?</h3>
        <ul>
          <li>More practical projects and problem-based learning.</li>
          <li>Better access to digital tools and technical training.</li>
          <li>Opportunities to learn throughout adulthood, not only in school.</li>
          <li>Teaching students to check sources and distinguish evidence from opinion.</li>
          <li>Workplaces that value competence, integrity and continuous improvement.</li>
        </ul>

        <h2 id="society">5. Social systems and leadership</h2>
        <p>
          Individual effort matters, but it cannot solve every structural problem. People
          need systems that are fair, dependable and capable of delivering public services.
          If opportunity depends heavily on personal connections rather than ability, talent
          can be wasted.
        </p>
        <p>
          Governments can support long-term development by improving education, infrastructure,
          public health, transparent administration, research and predictable rules for
          businesses. They can also create room for independent research and constructive
          criticism.
        </p>
        <p>
          Citizens, meanwhile, can demand evidence, participate in public life, respect
          different viewpoints and evaluate leaders by their actions and results—not only
          by promises or personal loyalty.
        </p>

        <h2 id="responsibility">6. Shared responsibilities</h2>
        <h3>Governments and public institutions</h3>
        <p>
          Invest in people and essential infrastructure, set transparent rules, measure
          outcomes and make public institutions accountable.
        </p>
        <h3>Businesses and employers</h3>
        <p>
          Create productive jobs, train workers, support innovation, follow fair practices
          and invest in skills that increase long-term value.
        </p>
        <h3>Schools, universities and educators</h3>
        <p>
          Encourage curiosity, practical learning, scientific reasoning, creativity and
          the ability to learn independently.
        </p>
        <h3>Individuals and communities</h3>
        <p>
          Build useful skills, question unreliable information, cooperate with others,
          take responsibility where possible and contribute to solving shared problems.
        </p>

        <h2 id="roadmap">7. A practical roadmap for preparing for the future</h2>
        <p>
          Progress becomes easier to discuss when broad ambitions are translated into
          practical steps. The following is a starting framework, not a universal formula.
        </p>
        <ol>
          <li><strong>Identify local needs:</strong> use reliable data to understand the most urgent barriers.</li>
          <li><strong>Strengthen basic foundations:</strong> improve education, health, infrastructure and digital access.</li>
          <li><strong>Develop relevant skills:</strong> connect training to real problems and employment opportunities.</li>
          <li><strong>Support experimentation:</strong> make it possible to test ideas, learn from failure and improve them.</li>
          <li><strong>Measure outcomes:</strong> track whether policies and programmes actually improve people's lives.</li>
          <li><strong>Keep the process inclusive:</strong> ensure that rural communities, low-income families, women and people with disabilities are not excluded from opportunity.</li>
        </ol>

        <h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion: preparing for tomorrow starts today</h2>
        <p>
          Humanity's ambition to live beyond Earth invites us to think on a long time scale.
          But a future-ready society is not defined only by rockets, artificial intelligence
          or advanced technology. It is also defined by how it educates people, creates
          opportunity, solves shared problems and holds powerful institutions accountable.
        </p>
        <p>
          Developing countries do not need to follow one person's path or copy another
          country's model exactly. They can identify their own strengths, understand their
          challenges and build systems that help more people contribute to progress.
        </p>
        <p>
          The question is not simply whether humanity will reach another planet. It is
          whether we will develop the knowledge, responsibility and cooperation needed to
          create a better future—on Earth and, one day, beyond it.
        </p>

        <h2>Frequently asked questions</h2>
        <h3>Why is Elon Musk interested in making humanity multiplanetary?</h3>
        <p>
          Musk has described making humanity multiplanetary as a long-term goal. The
          technical feasibility, timeline and practical value of a permanent settlement
          remain subjects for ongoing engineering, scientific and public discussion.
        </p>
        <h3>What does it mean for a country to be ready for the future?</h3>
        <p>
          It can mean having strong education, reliable infrastructure, economic opportunity,
          capable institutions, digital access and a population able to learn and adapt.
          The priorities differ according to each country's circumstances.
        </p>
        <h3>Can changing people's mindset solve development challenges?</h3>
        <p>
          Mindset can influence behaviour, but it is only one factor. Education, incentives,
          infrastructure, economic conditions and institutions also shape what people can
          realistically achieve.
        </p>
